The lighthouse keeper's journal, recovered from the north tower, begins in the middle of a sentence, as if the first pages had been torn out for kindling during some earlier winter. What remains covers eleven years of ordinary weather and one extraordinary season, and it is written in a hand that grows steadier as the years pass, which is the opposite of what you would expect from a man living alone on a rock two miles from the mainland.

He arrived in October. The boat that brought him also brought four crates of tinned food, a bale of lamp wick, a sack of coal that would not last the season, and a dog that belonged to nobody in particular and decided, somewhere between the harbour and the landing stage, that it belonged to him. He named the dog Tuesday, because that was the day it arrived, and because he had never been good at names. The previous keeper had left the tower in reasonable order, though the brass was dull and the logbook had been abandoned in August with a note that said only: the sea does what it wants.

The first winter taught him the rhythm of the place. The lamp had to be lit forty minutes before sunset and extinguished forty minutes after sunrise, and in between it had to be watched, because the clockwork that turned the lens had a habit of slowing when the temperature dropped below freezing. He learned to sleep in ninety-minute pieces. He learned that the fog signal, a compressed-air horn housed in a shed below the tower, could be heard by the fishing boats at four miles in still air and at barely one mile when the wind came from the southwest. He learned that the seabirds nested in the lee of the wall and that they would tolerate him if he moved slowly and never carried anything that glinted.

By the second year he had begun to record things that were not required of him. The official log wanted wind direction, visibility, hours of illumination, fuel consumed, and any vessel in distress. He gave it all of that, in the narrow ruled columns, and then he turned the book over and wrote on the back pages in a smaller hand. He wrote about the way the light moved across the water in the hour before dawn. He wrote about a pod of dolphins that came through the channel in June and stayed for six days, and how Tuesday barked at them from the rocks until his voice gave out. He wrote about the sound the tower made in a gale, which he described as something between a bell and a held breath.

The supply boat came every six weeks in good weather and every ten in bad. The men who crewed it were from the village on the headland and they were not talkative, but over the years they developed a routine of small exchanges. They brought him newspapers that were always three weeks old, which he read from front to back including the shipping notices and the advertisements for cough syrup, and which he then cut into strips for lighting the stove. They brought letters, though he received very few. They brought, once, a violin, which had been left at the harbour office by a woman who said only that it should go to the lighthouse. He never found out who she was. He taught himself to play badly and then, over four years, to play tolerably, and there is a passage in the journal where he notes that Tuesday had learned to leave the room at the first note and return, precisely, when he stopped.

The extraordinary season was the ninth year. It began with an unusually warm April, which brought the herring in early and brought the boats out with them, and it ended in a November storm that the meteorological office would later describe as the deepest low pressure recorded in that part of the coast in eighty years. In between there was a summer of remarkable clarity. Visibility of thirty miles was logged on eleven separate days. From the gallery he could see the mountains on the far shore, blue and folded, which in a normal year were visible perhaps twice.

It was during that clear summer that he began the survey. Nobody asked him to do it. He had noticed, over the preceding years, that the seabird colony on the eastern stacks was changing, and that certain species he had counted in his first seasons were now absent while others had multiplied. So he made a system. He divided the stacks into eleven sections by their natural fissures, he made a sketch of each section with the ledges numbered, and every morning for four months he counted nests, adults, and fledglings, and he entered the numbers in a ruled book he made himself by sewing pages together with lamp wick. He counted at the same hour each day, in the same order, from the same three positions, because he understood without having been taught that a measurement means nothing unless the conditions of measuring are held still.

The count came to nineteen thousand four hundred and six occupied nests at the July peak, distributed across seven species, and it showed a decline in two of them that was steep enough to be unmistakable. He wrote it all up in a letter, in his careful hand, and gave the letter to the supply boat. It went to the harbour office, and from the harbour office to the county naturalists' society, and from there, eventually, to a university, where a professor of zoology read it twice and then wrote back asking whether the keeper would be willing to continue the counts for a second season. He continued them for eleven, which is to say for the rest of his time on the rock and two years beyond it, from a cottage on the headland with a telescope in the window.

The November storm arrived with very little warning. The barometer, which he read four times a day, fell twenty-two millibars in nine hours, and he noted the fall in the log with an underline, which is the only underline in eleven years of entries. He secured the shutters on the service room, brought the dog inside, filled every container he had with fresh water, and lit the lamp two hours early because the sky had gone the colour of wet slate and there was no point pretending it was still daylight.

The storm lasted thirty-one hours. At its height the sea came over the gallery, which is one hundred and six feet above the mean high water mark, and the spray drove through a gap in the shutter frame and put out the stove. The clockwork jammed twice and he freed it twice, working by the light of a hand lantern with the whole tower moving under him in a way that he described afterward, with characteristic understatement, as noticeable. He kept the lamp burning for the entire thirty-one hours. Three vessels reported later that they had taken their bearing from it, and one of them, a coaster carrying timber, had been close enough to the reef that her master said in his statement that he had counted the seconds between flashes and steered by nothing else for two hours.

When it was over he went out onto the gallery and found the glass unbroken and the brass, which he had polished the previous week, entirely stripped of its polish by the salt. Tuesday, who was by then an old dog and mostly deaf, came out and stood beside him and looked at the water. The journal entry for that morning is four words long: still here. Lamp lit.

He stayed two more years. When the automation came, as everyone had known it would, he packed the violin and the sewn book of bird counts and the dog's blanket, and he left the logbook on the desk where he had always kept it, squared to the edge, with the last entry made in the correct columns and the pen laid across the page. The men who came to install the new equipment found it there and, not knowing what else to do with it, sent it to the harbour office, from which it eventually reached the county archive, where it sat uncatalogued for thirty-one years in a box marked miscellaneous coastal.

It was found by a graduate student who was looking for something else entirely. She read the front of the book first, the ruled columns, the wind and the visibility and the fuel, and she was ready to put it back when she noticed that the paper on the reverse side had been written on too. She turned it over. She read for six hours without moving from the chair, and when she came to the bird counts she went and found her supervisor, because eleven consecutive years of daily observations from a fixed position, taken by a single observer under a stable protocol, is not a diary. It is a dataset, and it was, as it turned out, the longest one of its kind in the entire region, spanning precisely the decades in which nobody else had been watching.
